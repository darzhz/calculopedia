Weight alone can't tell you whether you're fit. **Body fat percentage** can — and you can estimate it at home with nothing but a tape measure.

## The US Navy method

The most popular tape-measure formula estimates body fat from three or four circumference readings. Using **inches**:

    Men:   495 ÷ (1.0324 − 0.19077·log₁₀(waist − neck) + 0.15456·log₁₀(height)) − 450
    Women: 495 ÷ (1.29579 − 0.35004·log₁₀(waist + hip − neck) + 0.221·log₁₀(height)) − 450

## How to measure

- **Neck** — just below the larynx, widest point.
- **Waist** — men: at the navel; women: narrowest point.
- **Hip (women)** — widest point of the hips/buttocks.
- **Height** — barefoot, standing straight.

Keep the tape snug but not tight, and measure at the same time of day for consistency.

## Worked example (male)

Height **175 cm**, waist **85 cm**, neck **38 cm**:

    waist − neck = (85 − 38) ÷ 2.54 = 18.5 in
    height = 175 ÷ 2.54 = 68.9 in
    495 ÷ (1.0324 − 0.19077·log₁₀(18.5) + 0.15456·log₁₀(68.9)) − 450 ≈ 10.6%

That's in the **athletes** range for a man.

## What the numbers mean (ACSM categories)

| Category | Men | Women |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Essential fat | 2–5% | 10–13% |
| Athletes | 6–13% | 14–20% |
| Fitness | 14–17% | 21–24% |
| Acceptable | 18–24% | 25–31% |
| Obese | 25%+ | 32%+ |

Staying above *essential* fat is necessary for health; rising into the *obese* range raises metabolic risk.

## Accuracy note

The Navy method is within about **±3 percentage points** of lab measurements. Use it to track your direction over weeks, not to judge one reading. For precision, DEXA scans or calipers performed by a professional are more accurate.
