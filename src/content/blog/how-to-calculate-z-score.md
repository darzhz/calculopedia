A **z-score** (or standard score) answers one question: *how far above or below average is this value?* It measures distance in standard deviations, making different datasets comparable.

## The formula

    Z = (x − mean) ÷ standard deviation

- **x** — your data point
- **mean (μ)** — the average
- **standard deviation (σ)** — the spread of the data

## Worked example

A class mean is **70** with a standard deviation of **10**. What's the z-score of **85**?

    Z = (85 − 70) ÷ 10 = 1.5

85 is **1.5 standard deviations above the mean**.

## Reading the percentile

In a normal distribution, a z-score maps to a percentile — the share of values at or below it. A z of **1.5** is the **~93rd percentile**: about 93% of students scored at or below 85.

Common anchors:

| Z-score | Percentile |
| --- | --- |
| −2.00 | 2.3% |
| −1.00 | 15.9% |
| 0.00 | 50% |
| +1.00 | 84.1% |
| +1.50 | 93.3% |
| +2.00 | 97.7% |

## Quick interpretation

| Z-score | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| ≈ 0 | Average |
| +1 to +2 | Above average |
| +2 to +3 | Well above average |
| −1 to −2 | Below average |
| ±3 or beyond | Unusually far from the mean |

## Where z-scores shine

- **Standardized tests** — comparing scores across different test versions.
- **Quality control** — flagging parts that drift outside spec limits.
- **Research** — turning "apples and oranges" data into one common scale so it can be averaged or compared.
